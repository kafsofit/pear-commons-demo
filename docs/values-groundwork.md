# Augmentation not replacement: support for those who care

**Project-wide values groundwork · drafted from Alex K's direction on 6 November 2026**  
**Applies to:** pear, (s)pear, the practitioner commons, and the potential forum-like / wider civil-society horizon.  
**Status:** practitioner-led design commitment for discussion and refinement, not a claim of validated protection or effectiveness.

## Polished formulation

**“Augmentation not replacement” means augmenting the support available to workers—not augmenting their output, endurance, or exploitability, and not making them more dependent on a tool, an employer, or an infrastructure they do not control.**

pear aims to serve people at the forefront of crisis support and first response: those who provide care while carrying its relational and psychosocial demands. It should expand workers' room for preparation, reflection, skill development, connection, choice, and closure without weakening human relationships, undermining duty of care, or displacing the consent and agency of either workers or the people seeking support.

We use **psychosocial safety gear** as an aspirational metaphor: support that travels with the worker and helps them navigate demanding work, rather than infrastructure that turns the worker into a resource to be monitored, optimised, or consumed. Standalone or connected, for private self-journaling, skill development, or deliberately contributing to practitioner discussion and collective learning, pear should help care for those who care for others.

## Solidarity and collective power

**Building worker power and solidarity requires intentional care for ourselves and one another. Care is not a private add-on to labour or an individual obligation to become more resilient; it is part of how workers sustain relationships, recognise shared conditions, and build the capacity to act together.**

pear's tools can support that collective work by creating room for reflection, connection, shared learning, and mutual support. They cannot substitute for relationships, organising, collective bargaining, or the material conditions that make care possible. **Tools do not replace showing up or doing the work needed to shift culture, society, and power.** pear can accompany that work; it cannot perform it on anyone's behalf.

Nor may pear turn solidarity into a monitored workplace programme, map workers' relationships for management, or make participation in collective care a condition of receiving support.

The practitioner commons may help workers deliberately share practice knowledge and learn across differences, but it must not assume consensus or erase conflict. Solidarity requires room for dissent, accountability, boundaries, and the refusal to disclose. Its value lies in strengthening workers' relationships and collective agency—not in producing engagement metrics or making individuals easier to manage.

## What the metaphor must not imply

Safety gear does not make an unsafe workplace acceptable. It must not be used to transfer responsibility for protection onto individual workers, normalise excessive exposure, or substitute for staffing, rest, supervision, organisational accountability, fair conditions, and structural change. Nor does a digital support tool become proven protective equipment merely by adopting the metaphor.

**pear's protective value is a hypothesis to test with workers and affected communities, not an established clinical or occupational-safety claim.** Evaluation must consider burden, dependence, coercion, cultural fit, privacy, attention, relational quality, and whether the tool actually increases support—not simply whether workers use it or complete more work.

## Operational commitments

1. **Support, not extraction.** No throughput targets, endurance optimisation, worker scoring, employer surveillance, or expectations that a supported worker can safely absorb more harm.
2. **Capability, not dependency.** A worker must be able to decline, pause, leave, work more slowly, seek more human support, or continue without the tool without penalty or reduced access to care.
3. **Shared duties of care.** Supporting workers must not weaken the dignity, safety, agency, or support of the person seeking help. Worker benefit and support-requester benefit require separate evaluation.
4. **Consent with material alternatives.** Workers and support requesters need accessible information, meaningful choice, correction and challenge routes, and a human/tool-free path where relevant. Refusal must not degrade care. Consent does not justify unnecessary collection or secondary use; avoidable data flows should be declined.
5. **Support across the project's forms.** pear can stand alone; (s)pear remains conditional and separately governed; commons contribution is deliberate rather than extracted from private reflection. Connection must never be the price of basic support.
6. **Organisations retain responsibility.** pear cannot replace human colleagues, collective care, safe workloads, protected reflection time, non-punitive escalation, or accountable institutions.
7. **Care supports collective power.** pear may facilitate connection, shared learning, and mutual support, but must not map relationships for management, measure solidarity as engagement, or make collective participation compulsory. Workers retain room for dissent, boundaries, accountability, and refusal.

## A values test for every proposed feature

- Whose support, agency, and room for judgment does this expand?
- Could it be used to demand more output, longer exposure, greater availability, or more disclosure?
- What happens when a worker declines, pauses, needs help, or stops?
- What does the person seeking support know, choose, and retain control over?
- Does it shift responsibility for unsafe conditions from institutions to individuals?
- Does it strengthen workers' relationships and capacity to act together, or reduce solidarity to engagement, individual resilience, or managerial visibility?
- Who can dissent, set boundaries, refuse disclosure, or challenge the group without losing support?
- What evidence would show increased burden, dependency, relational harm, or inequitable benefit—and who has authority to stop it?

**Working test:** would the tool still protect a worker's dignity and choices if using it made them slower, less available, less legible to management, or more willing to seek human support?

The safety-gear metaphor and project direction originate in Alex K's raw draft.
